# My takeaways

This is our interpretation of the runs in this repository. It is opinion backed by data, not a
paper. Every number marked ✓ is regenerated by `python3 analysis/make_results.py` (see
[`results/RESULTS.md`](results/RESULTS.md)). Numbers marked ◇ come from our write-ups at the time
(`case-studies/*/RESEARCH.md`, `*_report.md`, `docs/runs/*.md`) and are not rebuilt by the script,
although the underlying outputs are all here. Paragraphs labelled **New reading** are analysis
added when this repository was assembled; they were not in our notes at the time.

Model: `mlx-community/Qwen3-8B-4bit`, run locally on Apple silicon through MLX, for every
simulated subject, persona and measurement. Runs date from 14 to 15 October 2026. No code here calls a
paid API: everything ran on one laptop. GPT Sol's experiment designs were written in a chat
session, not by code.

---

## What we were doing

We set out to replicate two packages that use language models to do social science, run them on a
small local model, push them onto problems they were not built for, and see where they break.

- **Expected Parrot** sells synthetic consumer panels: personas answer a survey about a product
  concept. We rebuilt its public "Lasagna Coke" study (12 personas, 3 segments, 7 questions) and
  asked whether a local Qwen panel reproduces its polarised result.
- **Automated Social Science** (Manning, Zhu & Horton, 2024) has a model act as the *scientist*,
  who picks the causes ("levers"), treatments and outcome for a short scenario, and then as the
  *subjects*, who act each treatment out. We gave Qwen the paper's four scenario seeds and let it
  design and run its own experiments; then we let a stronger model (GPT Sol) design the
  experiments and kept Qwen as the subjects.

## The conclusion

**I don't like how the levers these agents theorise are subject to stochasticity.** Which levers
the scientist proposes, and whether a lever then "works", both move with the draw. A pipeline that
reports "9 of 12 predicted directions confirmed" looks like evidence about levers. In our runs it
is partly evidence about which single draws happened to land in which cell.

The rest of this note is the evidence for that, and its limits.

---

## Takeaway 1: Whether a theorised lever "works" rests on single draws

Every cell in both Automated Social Science runs was executed once, at temperature 0.3. So each
lever's estimated effect is built from 27 single stochastic draws.

| | Qwen designs, Qwen subjects | GPT-Sol designs, Qwen subjects |
|---|---:|---:|
| levers theorised | 12 ✓ | 20 ✓ |
| scientist's predicted direction held | 9 of 12 ✓ | (predictions are prose; not scored) |
| \|coefficient / robust SE\| below 2 | 6 ✓ | 9 ✓ |
| verdict reversed by deleting 1 of the 27 cells | 2 ✓ | 0 ✓ |
| reversed by deleting 2 cells | 2 ✓ | 2 ✓ |
| reversed by deleting 3 cells | 0 ✓ | 4 ✓ |
| survives every deletion of up to 3 cells | 8 ✓ | 13 ✓ (+1 placebo with exactly zero effect) |

At the time we wrote that these were "one-draw local-model evidence, not human behavior" and that
the regression table carried "no significance stars" (◇). We said the 9-of-12 was not the strongest
result, because the same model proposed the hypotheses, played every subject and measured the
outcome, so agreement "can partly reflect a shared-model self-fulfilling loop" (◇).

**New reading.** The table quantifies how much of each verdict a single draw carries. Two of the nine
"confirmed" Qwen predictions (tax-fraud severity in bail, bidding strategy in the auction) reverse if
two of their 27 cells are dropped ✓; the fraud-severity effect is driven by a few very large bail
draws (maximum $500,000 ◇). Two of the three "failed" predictions (mediator presence, bidder risk
tolerance) would pass if one cell were dropped ✓. With GPT-Sol's designs, the fragile levers are the
three bidder valuations, seller attachment, cooperation and relevant experience ✓. The bidder
valuations are the lever auction theory says should matter most.

To be fair to the pipelines: the big, obvious levers are stable. Bail rises with prior convictions,
mug deals fall with the seller's reservation price, and interview ratings rise with communication
clarity under every deletion we tried ✓. The instability concentrates in the small and surprising
effects, which are the ones a "discovery" claim would rest on.

## Takeaway 2: Which levers get theorised depends on who draws them

The same four one-sentence scenarios produced three different sets of levers ✓:

| scenario | Manning et al. (GPT-4 + authors) | Qwen | GPT Sol |
|---|---|---|---|
| mug | budget, seller minimum, attachment | opening offer, budget, mediator | budget, reservation price, attachment, outside option, procedure |
| bail | criminal history, judge's caseload, remorse | fraud amount, criminal history, evidence | cooperation, priors, flight risk, community ties, evidence |
| interview | bar exam, interviewer friendliness, height | legal knowledge, clarity, confidence | bar status, reasoning, clarity, experience, height |
| auction | three bidder budgets | risk tolerance, starting price, strategy | three valuations, bid increment, information regime |

Across 44 levers, only two concepts (the buyer's budget, the defendant's criminal history) were
chosen by all three scientists ✓ (hand-coded concept matching; see `SHARED_CONCEPTS` in the script).
GPT Sol had read summaries of the paper, so its overlap with Manning et al. overstates independent
agreement (◇: "the planner was not blinded").

At the time our reading was that "the design changed before the results did" (◇). The paper's
designs were "more theory-anchored", Qwen's emphasised "intuitive behavioral labels that were
sometimes ambiguous" (◇), and GPT Sol produced "a richer experimental agenda" (◇).

**Caveat on this takeaway.** These are three different models, not three draws from one model. Qwen's
design stage drew exactly one design per scenario (all four valid on the first attempt ✓), and our
plan deferred "repeated scientist generations for stability and diversity" to a later stage (◇) that
we never ran. So this table shows that the lever set is not pinned down by the scenario. It does not
measure seed-to-seed variation within one scientist.

## Takeaway 3: Persona answers move between near-identical elicitations

In the Expected Parrot replication the same 12 personas answered the same four rating items under
three conditions ✓:

| comparison | ratings | identical | differ by 2+ points |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| separate calls: persona in the user prompt vs. in the system prompt (new seed) | 48 | 34 ✓ | 2 ✓ |
| one call with full context vs. separate calls | 48 | 22 ✓ | 11 ✓ |

The segment-level lever moves with it. Mainstream drinkers' purchase intent is 1.25 with full
context and 3.75 with separate calls ✓. That is the difference between "mainstream soda drinkers
reject this" and "mainstream drinkers would buy it as readily as foodies".

At the time we read the separate-call result as an elicitation artifact: once the product drops out
of the later questions, "respondents are rating an unspecified 'concept' based on identity rather
than the Lasagna Coke stimulus" (◇).

**New reading.** The first comparison is the closest pair we have: same personas, same questions,
same context loss. The persona moves into the system prompt (with one added line asking for a
decisive answer) and the seed changes, and 14 of 48 answers change ✓. Prompt wording and seed noise
are confounded in that pair, so it bounds the seed effect rather than isolating it.

## Takeaway 4: Fixed seeds make a run reproducible, not robust

Both codebases fix a seed for every call. Rerunning a cell replays the same draw exactly: the pilot
and the full run share 22 of 22 subject turns in the three unchanged frontier studies ✓, and the two
Expected Parrot replication runs gave 24 of 24 identical persona outputs ✓.

**New reading.** This is good engineering, and it makes every number here auditable. But it also means
that rerunning our code can never show the variance behind any single verdict. Only new seeds or
repeated draws per cell can. We did neither, which is the main gap in our evidence for this
conclusion (see "What we would do next").

## Takeaway 5: Expected Parrot's polarisation is mostly the personas, not the panel

| condition | appeal: adventurous / mainstream / health | purchase intent: same | purchase range |
|---|---|---|---:|
| our minimal personas | 4.25 / 2.50 / 2.00 ✓ | 3.25 / 2.00 / 1.75 ✓ | 1.50 ✓ |
| Expected Parrot's personas, one call | 4.00 / 1.75 / 1.75 ✓ | 4.00 / 1.25 / 1.00 ✓ | 3.00 ✓ |
| Expected Parrot's personas, separate calls | 3.25 / 1.50 / 2.00 ✓ | 3.75 / 3.75 / 2.00 ✓ | 1.75 ✓ |
| *public Expected Parrot run (cited)* | *4.75 / 1.00 / 1.00* | *4.50 / 3.00 / 1.50* | *3.00* |

- **Persona design explains much of the polarisation** (◇). Swapping in Expected Parrot's 12
  identity-rich biographies reproduced its 3.0-point purchase-intent spread exactly ✓.
- **Same average, different mechanism** (◇). Copying Expected Parrot's separate-call workflow matched
  its overall means (appeal 2.25 vs 2.25, clarity 3.83 vs 3.83 ✓) while the segments disagreed:
  Qwen was less enthusiastic among adventurous consumers and more willing among mainstream ones.
- **We did not reproduce the 4.75 vs 1.0 appeal split** (◇). Getting there with an 8B model would
  have taken outcome-leading instructions, which "would tune toward the published answer rather
  than faithfully reproduce the method".
- **The public run had a context-loss bug** (◇). With memory off, only the first question carried the
  concept and image, so later free-text answers were partly about nothing. Our pipeline sends one
  self-contained prompt per persona instead.

Remaining differences we could not remove: Expected Parrot used a vision model (Claude) and the
image; Qwen got a text transcription of the image (◇).

## Takeaway 6: A better scientist does not make better subjects

GPT Sol designed broader studies (five factors, several outcomes, a height placebo, information
treatments), and Qwen ran all 108 cells ✓. But the subjects did not follow the rules: in the
auction, 10 of 27 cells ended with no sale ✓, only 41% allocated the art to the highest-value
bidder ✓, and 3 winners paid more than their own private valuation ✓. Our reading at the time:
"scientific-planning capability and simulated-subject reliability are separate bottlenecks" (◇).

**New reading.** GPT Sol's first bundle also varied a fact the scenario fixes: its bail study had an
"Alleged financial harm" factor alongside a seed that says $50,000. It was replaced by "Post-offense
cooperation" before the full run (compare the two `design_bundle.json` files). Qwen made the same
move (◇: "varying fraud severity changes a quantity fixed in the scenario seed"). In both cases a
person caught it, not the pipeline.

## Things that went wrong, and stayed in the data

- **The first Qwen panel answered 3 to everything.** All 48 ratings in `lasagna-cola-mlx-12` are the
  midpoint ✓. A prompt fix against midpoint answers produced the baseline we report ✓. One sentence of
  instruction moved every segment mean.
- **The first frontier pilot measured nothing.** Every interaction finished, but parser v1 rejected
  all four measurements ✓ because Qwen returned evidence in a different shape (◇). The run is kept
  as `frontier-pilot-2026-10-14-schema-v1-failed/`.
- **Measurement failures.** Two of 108 all-Qwen measurements never parsed ✓; one GPT-Sol interview
  measurement failed three times and was recovered by a separate, logged call ✓.
- **The execution compiler needed retries.** Ten compiler calls were rejected before the four Qwen
  designs could run ✓ (an invalid two-person respondent in the mug study, malformed JSON in the
  auction) (◇).
- **Qwen's designs had substantive problems** that schema validation did not catch (◇): the mug
  design's budget treatment contradicted the buyer's fixed constraint, the auction varied the seed's
  stated starting price, and "rational" is not an ordinal level between conservative and aggressive.
  We ran them anyway, on purpose, because the generated design was the object of study.

## Limits

- **One subject model.** Every subject and measurement is Qwen3-8B-4bit. Nothing here is a claim
  about larger models, about "AI agents" in general, or about people.
- **One draw per cell, small n.** 27 cells per study, 12 personas per panel. That is why fragility is
  visible at all, and also why none of these numbers is precise.
- **No within-scientist repetition.** See Takeaway 2: the lever-set comparison is across models.
- **The planner was not blinded** and its planning step left no raw calls, only the design bundle.
- **Expected Parrot numbers are transcribed** from its public share page, not re-run. Its run cost
  $0.22 (◇).

## What we would do next

- Repeat each condition several times, or cross the same frozen designs with several subject-model
  families (◇: "the most informative next extension"). That is the direct test of the stochasticity
  claim.
- Draw repeated scientist designs per scenario from one model at fixed settings and measure how
  often the same levers come back (◇: deferred in our plan).
- For synthetic panels: human calibration and holdout concepts before trusting any simulated level
  or ranking (◇).
